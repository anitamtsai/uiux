Honors Thesis · Behavioral Decision Modeling

Modeling Why We Work While Sick

A decision-theoretic model of the sick leave decision, built by integrating the Health Belief Model into a utility framework and testing it across 4 worker personas and 3 illness types.

Role

Thesis Author & Sole Researcher

Tools & Methods

Decision Theory, Utility Modeling, Health Belief Model, One-Way Sensitivity Analysis

Timeline

Aug 2024 – May 2026

Organization

University of Michigan · Honors Cognitive Science Thesis · Advised by Prof. Michael Hayashi

Overview

Most sick leave research treats the decision as either purely structural (does the worker have paid leave?) or purely psychological (does the worker believe they're sick enough to stay home?). My honors thesis asked a different question: how do people actually weigh illness beliefs against real-world constraints when deciding whether to go to work sick? I built a decision-theoretic model that operationalizes the Health Belief Model as a utility function, then tested it across 12 persona-by-illness scenarios to see which factor actually drives the decision — not just which factors are correlated with it.

The Problem

Access to paid sick leave in the U.S. is fragmented by state and employer, and workers without it are more likely to work while ill, a behavior called presenteeism that drives disease transmission and worse health outcomes. But most existing research studies policy presence and aggregate outcomes, not how an individual actually integrates risk beliefs, perceived legitimacy, and cost into a single go-to-work-or-stay-home decision. Without a formal model of that decision process, it's genuinely hard to know whether education-based interventions (helping people recognize their illness is serious) or structural interventions (paid leave mandates) will actually change behavior for a given group of workers.

My Process

1

Frame the model

Operationalized the six Health Belief Model constructs — perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, self-efficacy, and cues to action — as terms in an additive utility function, where a positive total utility predicts staying home and a negative value predicts continued work attendance.

2

Design personas & scenarios

Built 4 worker personas (young/older × hourly/salaried) across 3 illness contexts (mild cold, panic attack, chronic back pain) — 12 scenarios total. Assigned each construct a different functional form (linear, convex, sigmoid, or step) per persona to reflect distinct cognitive processing patterns, such as a sigmoid function for mental-health severity to model a stigma-driven "legitimacy threshold."

3

Run scenario & sensitivity analysis

Calculated baseline utility for all 12 persona/illness combinations, then ran a one-way sensitivity analysis, perturbing each construct by +10% to identify which belief most moved the decision, and where the model sat close enough to zero for that change to flip the predicted outcome entirely.

4

Translate into policy implications

Interpreted where structural reform (paid leave mandates) versus cognitive or behavioral intervention (stigma reduction, managerial validation) would actually change behavior, based on which personas sat near their decision threshold rather than which constructs looked most influential on average.

Key Result

Across every illness type tested, employment structure — not illness severity — was the strongest predictor of the model's decision. Every hourly-worker scenario predicted continued work attendance; every salaried-worker scenario predicted staying home.

Takeaways

📊 Presenteeism is often rational, not irrational

The strongest predictor in the model wasn't illness severity — it was employment structure. That reframes "working while sick" as a constrained optimization problem, not a personal failure of judgment.

🎯 Sensitivity analysis tells you where to intervene, not what matters most

Most constructs were locally insensitive — a 10% change did almost nothing — except in the one case where baseline utility already sat near zero. The lesson generalizes directly to product or policy work: focus interventions where people are already on the fence, not where the average effect looks biggest.
